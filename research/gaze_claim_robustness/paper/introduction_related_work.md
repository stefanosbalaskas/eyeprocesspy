# Introduction and Related Work draft — CHI gaze-claim robustness study

## 1 Introduction

Eye tracking is widely used in HCI to make otherwise latent interaction processes empirically tractable. Researchers use gaze to study visual attention, search, reading, learning, decision making, and gaze-based interaction. Yet an eye tracker does not directly observe a fixation, an area-of-interest (AOI) visit, or a transition between interface regions. It produces a time series whose relationship to those constructs is established through a sequence of measurement decisions: how coordinates are represented, how missing or low-quality samples are handled, how eye-movement events are detected, how AOIs are delineated, which eye or binocular representation is analyzed, and how the resulting events are aggregated into a statistical outcome. Prior methodological work has shown that these stages are consequential. Event-detection algorithms can yield substantially different event durations and classifications (Andersson et al., 2017); AOI shape, size, and location can change gaze assignment (Hessels et al., 2016); and gaze accuracy and precision vary across users, targets, devices, and recording conditions in HCI settings (Feit et al., 2017). These are therefore not merely implementation details. They help define the behavior that is eventually modeled.

The broader reproducibility literature provides a language for this problem. Multiverse analysis makes reasonable data-processing alternatives explicit rather than presenting one processed dataset as inevitable (Steegen et al., 2016). Specification-curve analysis similarly asks researchers to define a set of theoretically justified, statistically valid, and non-redundant specifications and inspect how results vary across that set (Simonsohn et al., 2020). Many-analyst work has further shown that defensible analytic choices can produce materially different estimates for the same research question and data (Silberzahn et al., 2018). HCI and visualization researchers have begun to address the corresponding tooling problem through systems such as Boba and multiverse notebook workflows, which make alternative analyses easier to author, execute, and inspect (Liu et al., 2021; Sarma et al., 2023). Thus, neither analytical flexibility nor multiverse analysis is new to HCI.

Nor is analytical flexibility new to eye-tracking research. Peelle and Van Engen (2021) varied 8,281 temporal analysis windows in a visual-world study and showed that plausible window choices can materially change parameter estimates, including coefficient direction. More recently, Godwin, Lee, and Drieghe (2025) evaluated 1,890 reasonable cleaning and analysis pipelines for eye movements during reading. Their work directly establishes that eye-movement results can be studied as a multiverse and makes it inappropriate to motivate the present work by claiming that eye-tracking researchers have not considered alternative pipelines.

We instead focus on a different unit of robustness: the **substantive HCI claim produced by a cross-stage measurement pipeline**. A detector-comparison study can show that algorithms segment a signal differently without establishing whether a downstream interface or behavioral contrast changes. An AOI study can show that region definitions alter assignment without tracing that difference through event construction and a fixed inferential model. A reporting guideline can make a chosen pipeline reproducible without showing how the conclusion would behave under other defensible choices. Our concern is the propagation problem that connects these levels. When several upstream measurement choices are scientifically defensible, how much of that uncertainty survives to the effect estimate that an HCI paper interprets?

This shift from pipeline documentation to claim propagation matters because measurement decisions can interact. A velocity threshold expressed in degrees per second depends on how pixel coordinates are converted to visual angle; that conversion depends on display geometry and viewing distance. Event segmentation then determines which coordinates become fixations, AOI geometry determines which events count as being inside interface regions, and data-quality rules determine which trials reach the outcome model. Studying these choices one at a time may therefore miss combinations through which uncertainty propagates. Conversely, enumerating every imaginable preprocessing option would create an arbitrary multiverse of its own. A useful measurement-robustness analysis must define a finite, defensible decision set before inspecting the focal result and preserve the planned denominator when branches fail or become non-evaluable.

We operationalize this idea as a **claim-centered measurement-robustness workflow**. The workflow begins with a substantive claim contract, separates measurement-definition alternatives and sample-definition sensitivities from analyses that change the estimand itself, records a finite decision registry before focal-result inspection, propagates every planned branch through the same focal outcome/model contract, and retains failed or non-evaluable branches in the denominator. A branch is admitted only when its rationale is source- or literature-grounded, fully specified in advance, and executable without outcome-dependent tuning or silent repair. Robustness is then described multidimensionally: direction, magnitude, uncertainty, evaluability, sample support, and the decision families associated with variation. Specification frequencies are treated as descriptive properties of the declared universe rather than probabilities that a scientific claim is true. Here, “uncertainty” denotes a declared set of defensible measurement choices rather than a probabilistic model of sensor error. The intended audience is HCI researchers who derive event-, AOI-, or process-level measures from recorded gaze to support inferential claims about interaction. The workflow is not a universal prescription for real-time gaze-control pipelines or for studies whose measurement target requires a different claim contract.

We evaluate the workflow in two complementary empirical cases. The primary case reconstructs a between-AOI transition-rate outcome from sample-level gaze in an open self-regulated-learning (SRL) dataset (Juřík et al., 2025). We freeze 144 specifications spanning event detector, analyzed eye, viewing-distance assumption used for pixel-to-degree conversion, AOI boundary convention, quality rule, and cohort definition, while holding the Prompt/Non-prompt focal contrast, transition-rate outcome definition, and negative-binomial mixed-model contract fixed. Detector, eye, viewing geometry, and AOI branches alter measurement definition; quality and cohort branches are reported separately as sample-definition sensitivities because they alter the empirical support of the estimate. This case asks whether upstream uncertainty propagates to the downstream coefficient an HCI paper would interpret. The second case uses independent MCFW-Gaze data collected with a different tracker, nominal sampling rate, and set of interaction contexts. It does not replicate the SRL Prompt contrast. Instead, it tests whether detector-dependent event representations persist outside the primary dataset and how that disagreement relates to source-gaze quality.

We ask three research questions:

**RQ1 — Claim propagation.** How much can the direction, magnitude, uncertainty, and evaluability of a prespecified HCI claim vary across a declared set of defensible eye-tracking measurement and sample-definition decisions while the focal contrast, outcome-rate definition, and model term remain fixed?

**RQ2 — Sensitivity structure.** Which decision families are associated with the largest descriptive shifts in the focal estimate, and do consequential decisions interact rather than act independently?

**RQ3 — Independent measurement generalization.** Do detector-dependent event representations persist in an independent raw-gaze dataset collected with different hardware, sampling rate, and interaction contexts?

The paper makes three scientific contributions. First, we contribute a claim-centered workflow for propagating eye-tracking measurement uncertainty to a prespecified HCI claim while explicitly separating measurement-definition alternatives, sample-definition sensitivities, and analyses that change the estimand itself. Second, we provide an end-to-end empirical demonstration in which 144 prespecified specifications are propagated from raw sample representation through event detection, AOI assignment, quality/cohort rules, outcome construction, and the same focal model contract. Third, we provide an independent measurement-level validation showing how detector-dependent fixation representations behave across different hardware and interaction contexts without treating agreement as ground truth. These contributions are supported by a reproducible evidence package comprising decision registries, archive audits, specification manifests, failure accounting, workflow provenance, exact artifact hashes, and deterministic publication figures. The intended contribution is therefore not a new detector, a new AOI algorithm, or multiverse analysis itself. It is a method for making uncertainty in the **measurement-to-claim mapping** visible and auditable in HCI eye-tracking research.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Analytical flexibility and multiverse analysis

Scientific results can vary because reasonable analysis decisions are not uniquely determined by the research question. Steegen et al. (2016) formalized multiverse analysis around the observation that constructing an analysis-ready dataset involves choices about exclusion, transformation, and coding. Instead of concealing those alternatives behind a single dataset, their proposal evaluates the results obtained across reasonable processed datasets. Silberzahn et al. (2018) approached the same problem from a many-analyst perspective: independent teams addressing the same question with the same dataset used different defensible analytic strategies and obtained markedly different numerical results. These studies establish analytical flexibility as a source of variation that conventional sampling uncertainty does not capture.

Specification-curve analysis provides a complementary framework for making that variation inspectable. Simonsohn et al. (2020) describe three core steps: define theoretically justified, statistically valid, non-redundant specifications; visualize how estimates vary across them; and evaluate evidence across the specification set. Our workflow adopts the first two ideas strongly—finite justified decision sets and decision-linked result inspection—but differs in emphasis. We do not use the proportion of specifications crossing a significance threshold as a probability-like robustness score. We instead treat sign, effect magnitude, interval behavior, branch evaluability, and decision-family structure as distinct properties of a declared measurement universe.

HCI has also contributed systems for working with analytical multiplicity. Boba provides a domain-specific language and linked visualizations for authoring and reviewing multiverse analyses (Liu et al., 2021). Sarma et al. (2023) bring multiplexed alternative analyses into R notebook workflows at CHI. These systems address important authoring, execution, and sensemaking challenges. The present work is complementary: rather than introducing another general multiverse-authoring interface, we specify a domain methodology for eye-tracking measurement decisions whose scientific meaning and failure semantics must remain explicit as they propagate to an HCI claim.

### 2.2 Analytical flexibility in eye-tracking research

Eye-tracking data are especially suitable for sensitivity analysis because the path from recorded samples to the analyzed dependent variable contains several choices. Prior work has already demonstrated this problem directly. Peelle and Van Engen (2021) examined temporal-window selection in a visual-world paradigm. Across 8,281 windows, model coefficients and inferential outcomes changed substantially; even shifts within intuitively reasonable temporal ranges could reverse coefficient direction. Their recommendation is not a universal window but greater transparency, preregistration, and multiverse exploration.

Godwin et al. (2025) extend explicit multiverse analysis to cleaning and analysis choices in eye movements during reading. They evaluate 1,890 pipelines around the word-frequency effect, demonstrating that the eye-movement field already has concrete methods for asking whether a known effect depends on reasonable cleaning and analytic decisions. This work is the closest conceptual precedent to our primary case and defines an important novelty boundary. We do **not** claim to introduce multiverse analysis to eye tracking.

Our extension changes the scope and unit of interpretation. The SRL analysis starts earlier in the measurement chain—with sample-level gaze and uncertain visual-angle geometry—and carries measurement-definition and sample-definition decisions through event construction, AOI assignment, quality/cohort rules, an auditable process outcome, and a fixed downstream coefficient definition. This allows us to ask not simply whether alternative cleaning/analysis procedures move an established eye-movement statistic, but where uncertainty enters the raw-to-construct pipeline and whether it changes the substantive HCI contrast. The independent MCFW case then tests measurement representation outside the primary claim dataset, separating general detector behavior from the idiosyncrasies of that contrast.

### 2.3 Event detection and the representation of eye movements

Fixations and saccades are algorithmically constructed events rather than labels supplied directly by the eye tracker. Andersson et al. (2017) compared ten event-detection algorithms against expert human coding and found that resulting event durations varied substantially by algorithm. They also showed that conclusions about detector performance depend on event type, stimulus dynamics, and evaluation criterion; in particular, selecting a single fixation-detection winner is difficult. This literature motivates treating detector choice as measurement uncertainty rather than assuming that a commonly used algorithm provides a universally correct segmentation.

Different detector families also encode different assumptions. Fixed velocity- and dispersion-threshold approaches have long been used to identify fixations and saccades, whereas adaptive approaches such as Nyström and Holmqvist (2010) adjust velocity criteria to signal properties. REMoDNaV develops an open adaptive classifier for static and dynamic stimulation and explicitly validates it on datasets with different stimulus types and data quality (Dar et al., 2021). Its methodology also illustrates a point central to our SRL case: angular velocity is not independent of geometry. Pixel-based gaze coordinates need a physical/display reference when they are interpreted in degrees of visual angle, and validation datasets document stimulus dimensions and viewing distance as part of that reference.

Accordingly, our detector branches are not framed as contestants from which the analysis will select a winner. They are alternative operationalizations within a frozen measurement universe. The same principle governs our independent MCFW validation: pairwise similarity indicates how closely algorithms represent the same source stream under the declared rules, not which representation is scientifically true.

### 2.4 AOIs and spatial measurement decisions

AOIs convert spatial gaze into semantically meaningful interface regions, but their boundaries can contain substantial researcher judgment. Hessels et al. (2016) describe an “area-of-interest problem”: researchers studying similar stimuli may use AOIs that differ in shape, size, and location, complicating comparison and altering assignment in the presence of measurement noise. Their work proposes a noise-robust solution for particular stimulus classes, but the broader lesson is that AOI construction is part of the measurement model.

The primary SRL dataset provides unusually strong—but not complete—constraints on this stage. Its descriptor reports four AOIs corresponding to slide quartiles, a 1600 × 900 presentation, and an AOI area of 359,550 pixels (Juřík et al., 2025). Exact mathematical quarters would be 800 × 450 = 360,000 pixels, whereas 799 × 450 equals the reported area exactly. We therefore froze two source-compatible boundary conventions before focal-result inspection rather than inferring a seam convention from the Prompt coefficient. The purpose of this branch is not to manufacture spatial uncertainty where none exists; it is to preserve an ambiguity already present in the source documentation. More generally, the workflow includes AOI alternatives only when there is a scientifically defensible boundary uncertainty.

### 2.5 Data quality, missing gaze, and reporting in HCI

Measurement robustness also depends on the quality of the source signal. Holmqvist, Nyström, and Mulvey (2012) argue that accuracy, precision, data loss, and related characteristics are central to the validity and comparability of eye-tracking research. Orquin and Holmqvist (2018) broaden this concern by cataloguing threats to validity across experimental design, preprocessing, analysis, and interpretation. These contributions caution against treating data quality as a single afterthought or assuming that a convenient exclusion threshold has universal methodological status.

The HCI literature provides direct evidence that eye-tracker performance is heterogeneous in realistic interaction settings. Feit et al. (2017) found more than six-fold variation in gaze accuracy and precision across users and targets and documented differences across trackers, lighting conditions, and screen regions. Their findings connect measurement quality to practical decisions such as filter parameters and interface target sizes. More recently, Grootjen, Weingärtner, and Mayer (2024) investigated blink-related challenges for eye tracking in interactive systems and showed how data-processing choices around blinks can affect quality and accuracy. Together, these studies make clear that the recorded gaze stream cannot be assumed to have uniform measurement properties.

Reporting standards address an adjacent but distinct problem. The community-developed minimal eye-tracking reporting guideline by Dunn et al. (2024; 2023 edition) specifies information needed for critical appraisal, reproducibility, and comparison of eye-tracking studies. We view such reporting as necessary infrastructure for measurement robustness: analysts cannot reproduce or perturb an assumption that was never documented. However, complete reporting of one selected pipeline is not equivalent to sensitivity evidence. A study can report its detector, quality criterion, screen geometry, and AOIs perfectly while leaving unanswered whether another defensible configuration would alter the substantive conclusion. Our workflow addresses that second question.

### 2.6 Positioning the present contribution

Taken together, prior work establishes the major components of the problem. Multiverse and specification-curve methods show how reasonable analytical alternatives can be made explicit (Steegen et al., 2016; Simonsohn et al., 2020). HCI research has developed tooling for authoring and inspecting multiverses (Liu et al., 2021; Sarma et al., 2023). Eye-tracking studies have demonstrated sensitivity to temporal-window and cleaning/analysis choices (Peelle & Van Engen, 2021; Godwin et al., 2025). Detector-comparison, AOI, data-quality, and reporting literatures show that event representation and spatial/quality decisions are themselves consequential measurement choices (Andersson et al., 2017; Hessels et al., 2016; Holmqvist et al., 2012; Dunn et al., 2024).

We build on rather than replace these strands. The methodological object in this paper is the **mapping from a declared eye-tracking decision space to the stability of a prespecified downstream HCI claim**. Three design choices distinguish that object. First, the decision universe crosses multiple upstream measurement stages while the focal contrast, outcome-rate semantics, and inferential model remain fixed; branches that alter sample support are labeled explicitly as sample-definition sensitivities rather than treated as proof of formal estimand identity. Second, the planned denominator is itself an auditable object: failed, non-converged, and non-evaluable branches remain visible instead of disappearing from the robustness summary. Third, measurement behavior is examined independently from the primary HCI contrast through a separate raw-signal validation case. These features allow robustness to be discussed as a structured property of a measurement-to-claim pipeline rather than as a search for the preprocessing configuration that produces the most desirable result.

The contribution should therefore be evaluated on whether this claim-centered workflow is useful, reproducible, and informative for HCI—not on an assertion that its constituent ideas are individually unprecedented.
